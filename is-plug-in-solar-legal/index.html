Is plug-in solar legal in my state?

Four state laws checked on official sources, compared side by side, and what to check if your state is not one of them.

General information, not legal or electrical advice. Texts checked on 2026-10-31 on official legislature and regulator websites (links at the bottom). We only list a state as verified after reading its enacted text. Laws and utility rules change: read the current text before you buy.

The short answer

Most US states have no law on plug-in ("balcony") solar, so your utility's interconnection rules apply. Those rules usually require an application before anything feeds power into your wiring. A few states have passed laws that let a certified device plug into an outlet without an interconnection agreement. Below are the four we have verified on official sources. They agree on the basics: a 1,200 W ceiling, a certified device, no net metering credit, and no utility approval or fee. They differ on details that matter in practice.

Three details are easy to miss. In Maine, a typical 800 W kit is above the 420 W limit, so it needs a licensed electrician, a dedicated circuit and a notice to the utility. In Virginia, the law starts on January 1, 2027, and the notice to the utility must come before you install. Virginia tenants whose unit is billed under a ratio utility billing system need the landlord's written approval. In Maryland, the law is already in force, the notice to the utility also comes before installing, and the limit is one system per meter.

Other states

News coverage in 2026 reports plug-in solar bills passed or signed in more states, among them Colorado, Connecticut, New Jersey, California, New Hampshire and Vermont. We have not yet read those enacted texts, so we do not summarize them here. Caps, effective dates and conditions differ between states, as Maine's 420 W tier shows. Until a state appears in the table above, treat secondary reports as leads, not answers.

How to check your own state

  1. Search your legislature's website (not a news site) for "plug-in", "portable solar" or "balcony solar" in the current and previous sessions.
  2. Open the enacted or chaptered text, not the introduced bill. Caps and conditions often change through amendments.
  3. Write down four things: the watt cap (per outlet, per home or per address), the certification standard, whether and when you must notify the utility, and the effective date.
  4. Check whether the law covers your type of utility (investor-owned, municipal, cooperative). If it does not, ask your utility for its small-generator rules in writing.
  5. No law in your state? Then your utility's interconnection rules apply. Do not plug in a grid-tied inverter before you have read them.
  6. Renters: get written permission for anything mounted on a balcony or railing, unless your state's law gives you a specific right (as Virginia's does for larger landlords) and you follow its notice procedure.

Is it worth it where it is legal?

None of the three laws pays you for exported power. Only the electricity you use while the panel produces lowers your bill. Run the numbers with your own price per kWh and daytime use before you buy.
