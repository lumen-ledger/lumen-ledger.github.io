Is plug-in solar legal in Utah?

Yes, within the limits of Utah Code 54-15-601 (in force since May 7, 2025). This page covers what the statute says, what it leaves out, and what to check before you buy.

General information, not legal or electrical advice. Statute text checked on 2026-10-30 on le.utah.gov (links below). Laws change, so read the current text before you rely on it.

The short answer

Utah was the first US state to write plug-in ("balcony") solar into law, with H.B. 340 (2025 General Session, "Solar Power Amendments"). A device that fits the legal definition can be plugged into a regular outlet without an interconnection agreement. A utility covered by the law cannot require its approval, charge a fee for the device or demand extra equipment.

What counts as a "portable solar generation device"

Utah Code 54-15-102(13) defines it as a moveable photovoltaic device that meets five conditions:

#Condition in the statuteWhat to check on a kit
amaximum power output of not more than 1,200 wattsThe inverter's rated output. The statute does not say whether this means panel (DC) watts or inverter (AC) output. A kit where both are 1,200 W or less leaves no doubt.
bdesigned to connect through a standard 120-volt AC outletA plug for a normal household outlet, not a 240 V or hard-wired connection.
cintended primarily to offset part of the customer's electricity consumptionSize it to your daytime use, not to export power.
dmeets the most recent version of the National Electrical CodeAsk the seller which NEC edition the product is designed to.
ecertified by Underwriters Laboratories or an equivalent nationally recognized testing laboratoryAsk for the listing of the complete system (lab, standard, certificate number) and look it up on the lab's website. Seeing "UL" in an ad does not mean the product is listed.

What the law gives you (54-15-601)

  • Exemption from the interconnection requirements of 54-15-106, and from signing an interconnection agreement under 54-15-103.
  • The utility (an "electrical corporation") may not require you to get its approval before installing or using the device, pay any fee or charge related to it, or install controls or equipment beyond what is built into the system.

What the law requires or leaves out

  • Outage safety: the device must include a feature that stops it from energizing the building's wiring during a power outage. Confirm this in the certification.
  • No net metering: the device is not subject to the net metering program. Expect to get nothing for power you send to the grid. Only power you use while the sun shines lowers your bill.
  • Utility liability: the utility is not liable for damage or injury caused by the device. Ask whether your renter's or homeowner's insurance covers it.
  • Landlords and HOAs: the statute does not mention them. It limits what utilities can require, not what a lease or HOA rule can forbid. Renters still need permission for anything attached to a balcony or railing.
  • Which utilities: the limits apply to an "electrical corporation". If a municipal utility or a cooperative supplies your power, ask it whether it follows the same rules.
  • Sunset review: le.utah.gov lists this section as affected by Utah Code 63I-1-254 on 2036-01-01 (Title 63I sets repeal and review dates). If you are reading this later, check that the section is still in force.

Before you buy: 6 checks

  1. The inverter's rated output is 1,200 W or less, and you know the panel total.
  2. It plugs into a standard 120 V outlet without an extension cord or adapter.
  3. You have the certification for the complete system from UL or another nationally recognized testing laboratory (NRTL), and you have verified it.
  4. Your utility is an electrical corporation covered by the law, or you have its small-generator rules in writing.
  5. Renters: you have written permission for the mounting from your landlord, and from the HOA if there is one.
  6. You have estimated payback with your own price per kWh and daytime use, counting exported power at $0.

Average residential price in Utah: 13.19 ¢/kWh (January-August 2026, preliminary; 12.84 ¢ in the same months of 2025), from EIA Electric Power Monthly Table 5.6.B, read 2026-10-31. Frequently asked questions

Is plug-in solar legal in Utah?

Yes. Utah Code 54-15-601 (H.B. 340, in force since May 7, 2025) exempts a portable solar generation device from the interconnection requirements of 54-15-106 and from signing an interconnection agreement. The device must fit the definition in 54-15-102(13): 1,200 W or less, a standard 120 V outlet, intended to offset part of your own use, built to the latest National Electrical Code and certified by UL or another nationally recognized testing laboratory.

Do I need to notify Rocky Mountain Power or get approval?

Not under the statute. An electrical corporation covered by 54-15-601 may not require its approval before you install or use the device, may not charge a fee for it and may not require controls or equipment beyond what is built into the system. The statute sets no notice requirement. If a municipal utility or a cooperative supplies your power, ask it in writing whether it follows the same rules, because the limits apply to electrical corporations.

What is the maximum wattage allowed in Utah?

1,200 watts of maximum power output (Utah Code 54-15-102(13)(a)). The statute does not say whether this means panel (DC) watts or inverter (AC) output, so a kit where both the panel total and the inverter rating are 1,200 W or less leaves no doubt.

Will I get paid for power I send to the grid?

No. Under 54-15-601 the device is not subject to the net metering program, so count exported power at $0. Only electricity you use while the panel produces lowers your bill. At the Utah average residential price of 13.19 cents/kWh (EIA, January-August 2026, preliminary), each kWh you use yourself is worth about 13 cents; your own per-kWh price can differ.

Can I use an 800 W balcony kit with a 240 V plug?

No. The definition requires a device designed to connect through a standard 120-volt AC outlet. A 240 V or hard-wired connection falls outside it, and the normal interconnection rules then apply. Plug the kit straight into the outlet, without an extension cord or adapter.

Does my landlord or HOA have to allow it?

The statute does not mention landlords or homeowners associations. It limits what utilities can require, not what a lease or HOA rule can forbid. Renters need written permission for anything attached to a balcony or railing.

Who is liable if the device causes damage?

Not the utility: 54-15-601 says the electrical corporation is not liable for damage or injury caused by the device. Ask your renter's or homeowner's insurer in writing whether it covers a plug-in solar device, and keep the certification documents.

Does the device need special safety features?

Yes. It must include a feature that stops it from energizing the building's wiring during a power outage, and it must be certified by UL or an equivalent nationally recognized testing laboratory. Ask for the listing of the complete system (lab, standard, certificate number) and look it up on the lab's website.
