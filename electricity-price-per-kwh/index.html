What do you actually pay per kWh?

Two numbers hide in every electric bill: the average price and the price of one more (or one less) kWh. Here is how to find both, plus the 2026 average for every state.

General information, not financial advice. State averages from the US Energy Information Administration (EIA), Electric Power Monthly, Table 5.6.B, released 2026-10-23, read on 2026-10-31. 2026 values are preliminary and EIA may revise them.

Two prices, two different questions

PriceHow to compute itUse it for
Average priceTotal bill ÷ kWh usedComparing months, comparing with the state average, budgeting
Per-kWh price(Total bill − fixed monthly charges) ÷ kWh usedWhat you save by using less: solar, efficient appliances, shifting use

Fixed charges (often called "customer charge", "basic service charge" or "facilities charge") stay the same whether you use 100 kWh or 1,000 kWh. Cutting your use does not cut them, so they do not belong in a savings estimate.

Worked example

A bill of $142.30 for 750 kWh, with a fixed customer charge of $12.00:

  • Average price: 142.30 ÷ 750 = $0.1897/kWh (19.0¢)
  • Per-kWh price: (142.30 − 12.00) ÷ 750 = $0.1737/kWh (17.4¢)

Using the average price in a savings estimate would overstate every kWh saved by 9.2% in this example.

Where to find the numbers on your bill

  1. kWh used: usually in a "usage" or "meter reading" box (current reading − previous reading). Check the number of days: billing periods vary, often between about 28 and 33 days.
  2. Fixed charges: a line with a dollar amount and no kWh next to it, often near the top of the delivery section.
  3. Per-kWh lines: supply (generation) and delivery (distribution) each have a rate per kWh. Add them up and compare with your result above; riders and fuel adjustments are often per kWh too.
  4. Taxes and fees: a tax charged as a percentage of the bill grows with use, so it belongs in the per-kWh price. A flat fee does not.

When one per-kWh number is not enough

  • Tiered rates: if the price changes above a monthly threshold, a kWh you save comes off the top tier you reach first. Use that tier's rate.
  • Time-of-use rates: solar saves during daytime hours. Use the rates of the hours when it produces, not the peak or off-peak rate alone.
  • Minimum bills: some utilities charge a minimum amount. Savings stop when you hit it.
  • Seasonal prices: summer and winter rates can differ. Compute the per-kWh price for one summer and one winter bill.
